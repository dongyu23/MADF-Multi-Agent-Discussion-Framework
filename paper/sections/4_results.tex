\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Inter-judge agreement is near zero (RQ1)}

Table~\ref{tab:agreement} reports pairwise agreement between the two
judges on the 32 experimental discussions (overall score). Rank
correlation is $\rho=-0.05$ with a mean absolute difference of 0.72
points on a 10-point scale. Per dimension, agreement is weak
(relevance $\rho=0.05$) and \emph{negative} on insight depth
($\rho=-0.23$): the two judges systematically disagree about which
discussions are deep. Both judges come from the same vendor but
different model families, so this is a lower bound on the disagreement
between genuinely independent judges; a second-vendor judge is being
added to the released artifact.

\subsection{Leniency offset, not self-preference (RQ2)}

Judges differ systematically in level: one judge scores higher than the
other on 69\% of discussions (mean offset $+0.39$, bootstrap 95\%
CI $[+0.13,+0.65]$, excluding zero; the mirror offset for the other
judge excludes zero symmetrically). Crucially, the offset persists in
cells where the judge did \emph{not} produce the discussion, so it is a
property of the judge, not a preference for its own family. Direct
self-preference estimates are small and statistically
indistinguishable from zero: $+0.05$ (CI $[-0.70,+0.70]$) and $-0.10$
(CI $[-0.40,+0.40]$). This decomposes the two mechanisms that the
standard mitigation (``use a different family'') conflates: changing
family removes self-preference, which was not the operative effect
here, while leaving the leniency offset untouched.

\subsection{Single-judge component conclusions do not replicate (RQ3)}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the ablation replication at five
repetitions per condition under the primary driver. Under the
higher-level judge, removing arbitration or jitter \emph{raises} the
score (Cliff's $\delta=-0.48$ and $-0.64$): the full system is ranked
\emph{below} both ablations. Under the other judge, point estimates run
in the opposite direction, consistent with the audited evaluation's
ordering but with small effect sizes ($\delta=+0.56$, $+0.08$). The
audited evaluation's headline claim---that arbitration and jitter
contribute $-0.92$ and $-0.80$---is reproduced by \emph{neither}
judge at anything like that magnitude.

\subsection{Same-machine replication: only the judge varies}

Table~\ref{tab:samemachine} holds transcripts fixed and varies only the
judge, on the secondary driver (three repetitions per condition). A
same-model judge---the structure of the audited single-judge
protocol---ranks the full system \emph{below} the no-jitter ablation
($\delta=-0.56$); the cross-model judge ranks it below the
round-robin ablation at the maximum effect size ($\delta=-1.00$: every
round-robin discussion outscores every full-system discussion). In
neither configuration does the full system lead. Combined with
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, no judge--driver combination reproduces the
audited evaluation's component ordering: the ranking is a property of
the judge, not of the system.

\subsection{Mechanism metrics: the framework operates as designed}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Judge-free metrics confirm the ablations manipulate what they claim.
Under the primary driver, the full system produces unequal speaking
counts (Gini $=0.10$), round-robin produces a fixed rotation (Gini
$=0.00$ at three rounds; $0.13$ at five), and removing jitter collapses
most of the asymmetry (Gini $=0.03$), confirming jitter as the
operative source of speaking inequality. Forced speech never occurs
under round-robin (by construction) and occurs under both other
conditions. The framework's speaking mechanism behaves as documented;
the instability documented above resides in the evaluation layer, not
the framework.
