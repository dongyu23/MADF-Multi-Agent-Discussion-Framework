\subsection{Inter-judge agreement is low and heterogeneous (RQ1)}

Table~\ref{tab:agreement} reports pairwise agreement between the three
judges on the 32 experimental discussions (overall score). No judge
pair reaches strong rank agreement: among the three analyzed judges
$\rho$ spans $-0.10$ to $+0.28$ and mean absolute differences run from
0.66 to 0.87 points on a 10-point scale, so two judges looking at the
same transcript typically differ by most of a scale point, and their
rankings can be uncorrelated or slightly negatively correlated. The
cross-vendor pairs behave no better than the same-vendor pair, so the
disagreement is not a within-vendor artifact. A fourth released judge
(a flash-class model of the driver's vendor, not analyzed in the main
text) reaches $\rho=+0.47$ with one of the three---still far from
strong agreement---and $+0.21$/$+0.26$ with the other two. Per
dimension, agreement on insight depth---the most subjective axis---is
negative for the two pairs involving the flagship judge ($-0.23$,
$-0.10$) and only $+0.33$ for the remaining pair: the judges
systematically disagree about which discussions are deep. A
parser-clean sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:quality}) leaves
one pair at $\rho=+0.61$ while the other two stay near zero, so we
report agreement as heterogeneous rather than uniformly low. Because all
judges are LLMs from two vendors, these figures describe disagreement
across model families within and between two vendors; we do not claim
they bound disagreement with human judges.

\subsection{Leniency offset, not self-preference (RQ2)}

The three judges form a leniency ladder
(Table~\ref{tab:leniency}): step-5-preview scores $+0.61$ above the
mean of the other two judges on the same discussion (bootstrap 95\% CI
$[+0.40,+0.80]$, excluding zero), deepseek-v4.1-flash scores $-0.62$
below it (CI $[-0.83,-0.42]$, excluding zero), and step-3.7-flash sits
at the midpoint ($+0.02$, CI $[-0.23,+0.26]$). The two extreme judges
thus differ by about 1.23 points on the 10-point scale for identical
transcripts. Crucially, the offsets persist in cells where the judge did
\emph{not} produce the discussion: restricted to transcripts driven by
another model, the offsets are $+0.38$ (CI $[+0.08,+0.68]$, $n=13$),
$-0.30$ (CI $[-0.55,-0.03]$, $n=19$), and $-0.62$ (CI
$[-0.83,-0.42]$, $n=32$). The offsets are therefore properties of the
judges, not preferences for their own family. Direct self-preference
estimates are small and statistically indistinguishable from zero:
$+0.05$ (stratified bootstrap CI $[-0.60,+0.75]$; exact permutation
$p=1.0$) and $-0.10$ (CI $[-0.50,+0.30]$; $p=0.83$) for the two judges
that also served as drivers, on four same-driver and four other-driver
discussions each (an exploratory, unblocked comparison). This
decomposes the two mechanisms that the standard
mitigation (``use a different family'') conflates: changing family
removes self-preference, which was not the operative effect here, while
leaving the leniency offset untouched. The non-detection of
self-preference is a power statement (four vs.\ four discussions), not
evidence of absence.

\subsection{Single-judge component conclusions do not replicate (RQ3)}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the ablation replication at five
repetitions per condition under the primary driver. The audited
evaluation claims that arbitration and jitter contribute $-0.92$ and
$-0.80$ (removing either component \emph{lowers} the score), i.e.\ the
full system should outscore both ablations. The same-model judge---the
judge is the driver, the single-judge protocol structure---ranks the
full system \emph{below} both ablations (Cliff's $\delta=-0.48$ and
$-0.64$): removing components \emph{raises} the score, the opposite of
the audited direction. The cross-vendor judge runs in the audited
direction on both contrasts ($\delta=+0.32$ and $+0.60$) and the
same-vendor sibling on one ($+0.56$; $+0.08$ on the other). The audited
magnitudes are reproduced by \emph{no} judge at anything like that
scale: the largest effect any judge reports is $\lvert\delta\rvert=0.64$,
and it points the other way. With five repetitions per condition no
contrast is significant after Holm correction across the six contrasts
(three judges $\times$ two ablations; correction applied post hoc; raw
exact $p$ from $0.11$ to $0.68$, adjusted $p\geq0.67$), so we read this
table as \emph{direction and effect size}, not as a significance test: the finding is that the direction of the
component effect is a property of the judge, not that any single
contrast is established.

\subsection{Same-machine replication: only the judge varies}

Table~\ref{tab:samemachine} holds transcripts fixed and varies only the
judge, on the secondary driver (three repetitions per condition). The
three judge identities produce three different condition orderings on
\emph{identical} transcripts: the driver's own model ranks the full
system below the no-jitter ablation ($\delta=-0.56$); the same-vendor
sibling ranks it below the round-robin ablation at the maximum effect
size ($\delta=-1.00$: every round-robin discussion outscores every
full-system discussion, exact $p=0.1$, the resolution floor at $3\times3$);
and the cross-vendor judge ranks the full system above the no-jitter
ablation ($\delta=+0.78$; interval for the mean difference
$[+0.20,+1.80]$ excludes zero, but exact $p=0.2$ and Holm-adjusted
$p=1.0$, so we treat it as suggestive only). Across both drivers and
all three judges, no judge--driver combination reproduces the audited
evaluation's component ordering on both contrasts. This is the
strongest form of the evidence available at this sample size: the
transcripts are held fixed, so the only changed factor is the judge,
and the ranking moves. We do not claim a driver-independent causal
effect beyond the tested transcripts; the factorial's driver and judge
factors are partially confounded by design (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Mechanism metrics: the framework operates as designed}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Judge-free metrics confirm the ablations manipulate what they claim
(Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). Under the primary driver, the full system
produces unequal speaking counts (Gini $=0.27$), round-robin produces a
fixed rotation (Gini $=0.00$), and removing jitter collapses most of
the asymmetry (Gini $=0.09$), consistent with jitter as the operative
source of speaking inequality under this driver. Under the secondary
driver the pattern does not hold (no-jitter Gini $=0.38$ exceeds the
full system's $0.23$), so we do not generalize the jitter claim beyond
the primary driver. Forced speech never occurs under round-robin (by
construction) and occurs at similar rates under the other two
conditions. Note that these Gini values count all three agents, with
zero-speech agents included; the audited pipeline's own turn-count
statistic omits zero-speech agents (\S\ref{sec:method}), which is the
defect whose robustness check is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:robust}. The framework's speaking mechanism behaves
as documented under the primary driver; the instability documented
above resides in the evaluation layer, not the framework.
