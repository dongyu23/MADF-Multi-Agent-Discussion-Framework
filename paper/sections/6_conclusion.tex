\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We audited the evaluation protocol by which a multi-agent discussion
system's component importance is established. Re-implementing the
speaking protocol as a headless testbed and scoring 32 discussions with
three judges from two vendors, we found that no judge pair reaches
strong rank agreement on discussion quality ($\rho\leq+0.28$ among the
analyzed judges); that the judges form a leniency ladder whose extremes
differ by about 1.23 points on a 10-point scale, persisting on
transcripts the judges did not produce, while self-preference is not
detected---refining the standard family-substitution mitigation; and
that a single judge's component-importance conclusions vary with the
judge (Cliff's $\delta$ from $-1.00$ to $+0.78$), with no judge
reproducing the audited evaluation's component ordering in the
same-machine replication and no judge reproducing its effect
magnitudes. With the available repetitions no contrast is statistically
significant after multiplicity correction; the finding is the
instability of the direction, not a significant difference.

The practical upshot is a reporting norm rather than a prohibition:
multi-judge scoring with agreement reporting, and conclusion-level
escalation when judges disagree.

\paragraph{Limitations and next steps.}
Four limitations bound these claims, each with the experiment that
would relax it. (i)~\emph{Scope}: one framework and, for the ablation,
one topic; a multi-topic, multi-framework replication is the direct
next step. (ii)~\emph{Statistical power}: five repetitions per ablation
condition (three in the same-machine replication) limit resolution, and
LLM sampling adds run-to-run variance; larger repetition counts and
repeated runs at fixed seeds would tighten every interval. (iii)~\emph{No
human calibration}: we show that judges disagree with each other, not
which judge (if any) is right; human annotation of a transcript subset
is the calibration step and is the backbone of our companion study on
speaking-allocation policies. The judges' forced 1--10 integer
elicitation is itself a documented validity risk on indeterminate
rating tasks~\cite{guerdan2025validating}, which human calibration
would also bound. (iv)~\emph{Judge provenance}: the judges
span two vendors but include only one cross-vendor model, and the
audited framework's own evaluation used a third vendor's judge, so our
figures describe disagreement across a two-vendor slice of model space;
a wider judge panel is a stated extension of the released pipeline. The
judge-free mechanism metrics and the released artifacts (code,
transcripts, decision logs, judgments, robustness re-judgment, jury
record) make every number in this paper checkable. Immediate next steps
are a wider judge panel at scale, human-annotation calibration of the
judge-free metrics, and a multi-topic expansion---the last two are the
substance of our companion study on speaking-allocation policies, where
these metrics serve as the evaluation backbone.

\paragraph{Ethics and reproducibility.} The first author developed the
audited framework; the audit targets the evaluation protocol, and the
repository contains the protocol-fidelity mapping, all prompts, all
transcripts with per-round decision logs, all raw judgments, and the
analysis scripts. No human subjects were involved; all agents are
LLM-driven with public-persona skill files.
