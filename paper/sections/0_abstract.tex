Multi-agent discussion systems let several role-conditioned agents
deliberate on a question, and their evaluation increasingly relies on a
single large language model (LLM) acting as judge: the judge reads the
discussion transcript and scores quality dimensions, and the resulting
scores drive claims about which system components matter. The robustness
of such claims to the identity of the judge has not been examined. We
audit this evaluation protocol on \sysname{}, a decentralized roundtable
framework in which each agent bids for the floor with a self-assessed
confidence score. We re-implemented the framework's speaking protocol as
a headless, dependency-free testbed, and scored 32 discussions (one
warmup discussion excluded) with three judges drawn from two vendors:
two models of the driver's vendor and one cross-vendor model. Three
findings emerge. First, rank agreement between judges is low and
heterogeneous (Spearman $\rho$ from $-0.10$ to $+0.28$ among the
three analyzed judges; $+0.47$ at most including a fourth released
judge), and mean absolute differences run up to 0.87 points on a
10-point scale; in a parser-clean sensitivity analysis one cross-vendor
pair reaches $\rho=+0.61$ (moderate) while level differences remain
large. Second, the judges form a leniency ladder---one scores
$+0.61$ above the two-judge mean and another $0.62$ below it, both with
bootstrap confidence intervals excluding zero, and the offsets persist
on transcripts the judge did not produce---while self-preference is not
detected; this refines the standard advice that using a different judge
family suffices. Third, component-importance conclusions drawn from a
single-judge ablation vary with the judge (Cliff's $\delta$ from $-1.00$
to $+0.78$ across judges and drivers), and in a same-machine replication
where only the judge varies, no judge reproduces the audited
evaluation's component ordering on both contrasts; no contrast survives
multiplicity correction, so we report direction and effect size rather
than significance.
We conclude that single-judge LLM scores do not support
component-importance claims about multi-agent discussion systems in
their current form, and we propose a case-study-derived multi-judge
reporting heuristic. All code, transcripts, judgments, a robustness re-judgment of the
discussions affected by a defect we found in the audited judge prompt,
and an auxiliary model review record are released with the paper.
